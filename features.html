Features

What the crew actually does.

No mystery AI claims. Every line below is shipped, in beta53, and most of it is covered by a test you can run.

The flagship

The missile countdown

The server hook streams every in-flight weapon. WILCO measures the closure on the one guiding on you and speaks in bands: warn, crank at twelve seconds, BREAK at six — then the call that saves your energy: “missile trashed, you're clean, come back left.”

12s“Defend — missile inbound, right three o'clock, twelve seconds.”
08s“Crank left. Eight seconds.”
04s“BREAK RIGHT — FLARES — NOW.”
—“You're clean. Resume tasking.”

The brain

Deterministic. Offline. 33 milliseconds.

Exact math, not vibes

BRAA, pigeons, vectors, nine-lines — computed from the live feed, never invented. If the feed doesn't know, WILCO says so. Every number you hear can be checked against the mission.

OVERLORD> BRAA 042 / 18 / 22,000 / HOT

bearing · range · altitude · aspect — measured, not guessed

No API keys, ever

The default brain runs entirely on your PC. Optional cloud AI exists for richer improvisation — the offline brain feeds it the facts so it can't hallucinate.

The prediction engine

It flies the world forward: SAM ring entry warnings before you cross, merge countdowns, glideslope deviations, fuel playtime.

Readback verification

Runway, heading, altitude, squawk — checked in code. “Negative, runway three five right — read back.”

Mission memory

Kill tally across the shift, groups that faded and came back — “that group we lost is back.” Joker fuel remembered and nudged.

Wrong-net armor

Call the wrong frequency in any words and you're pointed at the right station, with its freq spoken — even when the call itself wasn't understood.

Ships and naval groups

Every hull in the mission is on the scope, so Nail can work you onto a naval group instead of calling the water empty.

The ears

It understands you, not your spelling.

102 intents, 42,039 phrasings

Say it formally, casually, or half-swallowed at 2 AM — no phrases to memorize. Certified on exams built from sentences it has never stored, re-run before every release.

0.0% of 100k natural asks
0.0% of the 1M-utterance exam

Garble repair

Damerau–Levenshtein healing over an aviation lexicon: “pichure” → picture, “bogey dop” → bogey dope. Every recorded real-world mangle understood, and 96% still right with one character in twenty corrupted.

Safety gate

Clearances are anchor-gated and confirm-tiered. Chatter can never trigger a takeoff clearance — hard-asserted in the test suite.

It learns YOUR phrases

Miss → rephrase → learned forever, per pilot. Your personal wording becomes part of its vocabulary.

Sub-second endpointing

It knows when you're done talking — and answers standard calls in about 0.9 seconds, mic to voice.

Compound calls

“Picture and pigeons” gets both answers in one transmission.

The sentence after

Follow-ups know what you were just talking about — “how far is it now?”, “say again the altimeter”, “and my squawk?” Ask a question while a clearance is outstanding and you get an answer, not a readback correction.

Faster ears on NVIDIA

With a CUDA card it runs a bigger speech model at roughly 200 ms a transcription instead of 650 — fewer “say again”s with the mission running.

The voice

Sounds like a radio, not a robot.

A real comm-radio chain

Band-pass, presence peak, AGC, envelope-riding hiss, squelch tail — rebuilt from how radios actually distort, not a robot voice with static pasted on.

Different person per position

Ground doesn't sound like Tower. Overlord doesn't sound like Nail. Offline neural voices, no cloud.

Voice mood

Urgent calls speed up. Quiet nights relax. The pacing follows the situation.

Voice Studio

Paste an audio clip, train, assign — put any voice you own on any position, all locally.

Crew packs

One pick swaps the whole crew: every voice, the AWACS and JTAC callsigns, and the manner they work you in — by-the-book, or a looser squadron net.

The instructor

Say “teach me”. The crew teaches.

Guided lessons, on frequency

Three lessons taught by the controllers themselves — the full airfield pattern, working AWACS, working the FAC. Every step prompts the exact words, checks your call, and moves you to the next one. The lesson follows you across frequencies, Ground to Tower and back.

The radio report card

The crew grades your radio each session — and Ground speaks it when you taxi in: “Radio tonight: 9 of 10 first time — sharp.”

A crew that remembers you

Next check-in, the controller knows: “sharp radio last time out.” A rough night gets the honest offer to drill the calls.

Misses become teaching

A wrong call mid-lesson gets the exact words, not a “say again”. Outside lessons, a near-miss gets a named guess: “if that was wind check, just say ‘wind check’.”

Shown round on day one

The first time you open WILCO a recorded voice walks you through the Control Station — pointing at each control as it talks, and opening the actual kneeboard and career pages rather than describing them.

The net

Disciplined with twelve pilots. Alive with one.

Mic triage

Every pending call is ranked — the pilot closest to dying gets the frequency first. The crew never steps on a human: the freq is re-checked the instant before it keys up.

The living net (solo)

Real AI traffic in your mission gets worked around you. Auto-off the moment a second human checks in.

One shooter at a time

Nail holds your wingman high in cover until you're off target — then calls him in.

Handoff memory

Tower already knows what you told Ground. The shared flight log follows you across positions.

Real ATIS

Information letters only advance, like real life — and controllers catch you reading back a stale one.

Worked as a flight

Check in as a two-ship or a four-ship and Ground and Tower work you as one — “Warhawk flight, cleared for takeoff” — not as separate aircraft.

Busy-net standby

Key up mid-clearance to another pilot and you get a “standby”, then they come back to you. An emergency is never told to wait.

The warning reaches you

Every controller has its own frequency and you can only listen to one. A missile in the air is called by whoever is working you — not only by the senior net, on a radio you left ten minutes ago.

The paperwork

The card you fly with.

A real data card, six pages

The sortie, the nav log, comms, loadout, threats and the brief — laid out the way an aircrew data card has looked for fifty years. Grey box means a label, yellow box means a value. Click any value and type into it.

Pull the flight plan

The card fills itself in from the mission you loaded: every waypoint, the runway that is into the wind, your loadout station by station, and the rest of the package with their frequencies.

It does the leg work

Headings, leg distances, elapsed times and a fuel ladder down every leg — worked out for you, and worked out again the moment you move a waypoint.

The pages you reach for

Known threats and their rings, every frequency the crew answer on, codewords and authentication — on the card, not in another window.

Straight onto your knee

Build puts the deck on your in-game kneeboard at RShift+K, and it can rebuild itself while you fly. Export saves the pages anywhere you like — to print, to share, or to drop into a mission's own kneeboard folder.

Your record

A crew that knows who you are.

The logbook is kept for you

Hours flown, sorties, landings and how smooth they were, kills split air-to-air, ground and air-defence — all counted where the facts are proven, never guessed from words.

It remembers you

A controller who has worked you before greets you like it — your landings, your fuel habit, your kills. Once, on check-in, then it gets on with the job.

Career dossier

Your record drawn as one card: rank from Rookie to Ace, landing trend, radio proficiency, the crew's own notes on how you fly.

Head to head

Compare your record against anyone else the crew has worked — hours, kills, touchdown rate, radio — and a squadron board of everybody on the books.

On your kneeboard

The dossier and a deck of reference pages install straight into the DCS kneeboard, so your career and every call ride on your knee at RShift+K.

Honest limits

What it doesn't do (yet)

  • Carrier ops has a text-mode prototype — it's not in the Control Station yet.
  • One controlled airfield at a time (a second field's crew can run in parallel).
  • Windows only — it lives next to your DCS install.
  • English radio. It forgives accents; it doesn't translate.
